    What this is

    Type Odia the way it sounds, in English letters. Unlike a keyboard, it shows what each word means before you pick it. Nothing you type leaves this device.

    What to type

    Use Up and Down to choose a suggestion, then Space to take it and start the next word, or Enter to take it and start a new line. Tab takes it without a separator. Number keys 1–9 take a numbered suggestion, as on Microsoft keyboards (turn this off in Settings to type digits straight after letters). Alt (Option on Mac) plus 1–9 always takes it. Left and Right move through your text. Undo with Ctrl+Z or Command+Z; redo with Ctrl+Shift+Z or Command+Shift+Z.

    Links, email addresses and identifiers such as PCR2 stay as typed. Pasted text stays as pasted. Choose “Keep” to keep an English word. Escape only closes the suggestions for the current word. Copy finishes the current eligible word visibly, then copies the text in the editor. In Meanings, one search box finds everything: an Odia word, an English word (water), or how you type a word (kemiti) when you are not sure of the matras. Insert Odia places a result at your last editing position.

    Case is ignored for ordinary letters, so ta and Ta behave the same. Where a spelling is ambiguous the word is underlined with dots — tap it and pick the one you meant. Your choice is remembered on this device.

    About ଡ଼. This letter is everywhere in Odia (ଓଡ଼ିଶା, ଓଡ଼ିଆ, ବଡ଼) so you should not have to work for it. Common words are recognised from the dictionary, and elsewhere a d inside a word offers it as an alternative. Type R if you want it outright.

    Conjuncts form on their own — type consonants back to back and the halant is inserted for you. namaskaara → ନମସ୍କାର, ksha → କ୍ଷ.

    Exact spelling (Settings, Advanced) turns off guessing entirely: one roman spelling, one Odia output, no suggestions. It follows the ITRANS scheme. Useful when you know exactly what you want.

    Typing Sanskrit

    Two modes. Everyday is the default — ordinary Odia leads, Sanskrit spellings sit a slot or two down. Śloka (verse) flips the ranking for verse: a word-final ଂ beats ମ, ଳ beats ଲ, and the Sanskrit vocalics become typeable. The original engine, without Sanskrit forms, is Classic engine in Settings, Advanced.

      Conjuncts Sanskrit needs

      Two families that the base scheme could not reach. A nasal before a stop assimilates in the script — ଙ before velars, ଞ before palatals — but nobody types it that way, so a plain n works.

      Nasal + stop ଙ୍କ nkଙ୍ଖ nkh ଙ୍ଗ ngଙ୍ଘ ngh
      ଞ୍ଚ nchଞ୍ଛ nchh ଞ୍ଜ njଞ୍ଝ njh
      Subjoined ୱ ସ୍ୱ sv sw ଦ୍ୱ dv dw ତ୍ୱ tv tw ଶ୍ୱ shv

      Why ୱ only in conjuncts. Sanskrit व is written ବ standing alone (ଦେବ, ବିଷ୍ଣୁ) but ୱ when subjoined — ସ୍ୱାମୀ outnumbers ସ୍ବାମୀ more than three to one in real Odia text. There is no rule context for "after a halant", so these are spelled out as their own keys.

      Why ଳ so often. Sanskrit ल in an Odia tatsama word is usually ଳ, not ଲ: ଜଳ, ଫଳ, ବଳ, କାଳ. Measured across the whole corpus, ଳ wins 2.4 to 1 at the end of a word. Śloka mode ranks it first; the other modes offer it one slot down.

      The base scheme is in docs/romanization-reference.pdf and these additions are in docs/romanization-reference-v2.pdf. Both are drafts unreviewed by a native speaker — every mapping is a proposal, and a wrong one is a line in a TOML file, not a code change.
